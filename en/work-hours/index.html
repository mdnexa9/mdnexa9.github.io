Practical guide

Every hour you work should be written down.

If you are paid by the hour or by the day, you rarely lose money because you worked less. You lose it because when the numbers are questioned, you have no numbers to show. Here is how to keep a log that takes a minute a day and turns the month into a sum instead of an argument.

1. The problem is not memory, it is the gap

At the end of the month everyone remembers the last figure and nobody remembers the details. “I worked three and a half days that first week” is a true sentence, but it proves nothing when the other side says “two days”. The difference between those two versions is real paid work, and all of it disappears because it was never written on the day it happened.

A log is not an accusation. It is simply what turns the monthly account into addition instead of debate. And people who write every day notice things that otherwise slip past: the week with more hours than expected, the transport money never counted, the half day that was never paid.

2. What to write each day, exactly

A useful log is short: six facts, one minute at the end of the day.

The date, written out — not “yesterday”.

The type of day: full day, half day, part of a day, or day off. This single field prevents half of all mistakes, because most disputes are not about hours but about how a day is classified.

Start and end time, or the total hours, including any break.

The rate you agreed for that day or that hour — not the rate you remember a month later.

What you actually received, and when. The gap between earned and received is the most important number in the whole log, and it is exactly the one nobody remembers.

Expenses, if you pay for transport or tools out of your own pocket.

If you are paid hourly, count half days and partial days the same way every time: a half day is half the day rate, and part of a day is proportional to the hours in a full day. The arithmetic, with examples, is in how to calculate daily pay.

3. Notebook, spreadsheet, or app

The notebook: no battery, no connection, and perfectly adequate. Its limitation is that it lives in one place and the totals have to be added by hand at the end of the month.

The spreadsheet: excellent if you work at a computer. Most hourly workers record from a phone, right after a shift, and a file living on another machine gets updated late or never.

The app on your phone: its advantage is being there at the moment, with totals that add themselves up. The flaw in many apps is that they require an account, a connection, or put your records in the cloud instead of on your device.

5. Make the log work for you

A written log sits in your phone and changes nothing until you use it. Two small habits do the work:

Send the monthly summary. One image of the summary to the person paying you, before payment day. The numbers become something you both agreed on rather than a surprise.

Keep what confirms the numbers. Messages about shifts, bank transfer notifications, any signed sheet. The log puts them in one sequence, and that sequence is what makes your position clear if payment is late.

6. Four mistakes that repeat

Rebuilding the whole month from memory at the end; confusing what was earned with what was received; skipping half days because they are “small”; and never recording days off, so the month looks emptier than it was. Each of those costs real hours.

Frequently asked

Is a notebook enough?
Yes, if you write in it daily. What counts is the day you record, not the tool — but the notebook stays at home while your phone does not.
I skipped a week — is it too late?
No. Rebuild from messages, transfers and days off you remember with confidence, write only what you can support, then go back to daily entries.
Do I need a lawyer to use it?
No. In most cases a clear summary settles the account; if it stays open, an advice service or union can help.
Does it work offline?
Yes — no account, no connection, data stays on the phone and can be exported.
Does it work if I am paid weekly?
Yes: record the week as the unit and enter the agreed weekly rate. The structure is the same.
