Guide · Work hours tracker

A work hours tracker you will actually keep.

Most people who lose pay do not lose it because they worked less. They lose it because when the month ends, nobody can say how many hours were really worked. A tracker fixes that in about a minute a day — if it records the right fields, in the right order, at the right moment.

What a work hours tracker has to contain

A tracker is only as useful as its fields. Six of them cover almost every dispute you will ever have about pay:

1. The date. Written out, not “Monday” and not “yesterday”. Months of entries must be readable by someone who was not there.

2. The type of day. Full day, half day, part of a day, day off. This one field prevents most errors, because the argument is rarely about hours — it is about how a day was classified.

3. Start and end time, or the total hours when you cannot be precise. If you record times, record the break too.

4. The rate that applied that day. Not the rate you remember at the end of the month. Rates change, and so do the agreements around them.

5. What you were actually paid, and the date it arrived. Earned and received are two different numbers, and the gap between them is the most important figure in the whole record.

6. Expenses you paid yourself — transport, meals away, tools. They are not hours, but they belong in the same month.

The one-minute routine

Tracking fails when it becomes a weekly task. Do it at the end of the shift, in the same place every day, before you leave the building or the site: open the app or the notebook, enter the date, the type of day, the hours, and close it. That is the whole routine.

Then once a week — Sunday evening works for most people — open the week and check three things: every working day has an entry, the total matches what you expected, and any half day is marked as a half day. Five minutes now saves an hour of reconstruction at the end of the month.

If you forget a day, rebuild it the same week from messages, transport tickets or shift notes, and mark the entry as reconstructed in your head. Never invent a time you do not remember: write the range you are confident about, because a modest, accurate record is worth more than a precise-looking guess.

Breaks, split shifts and overtime

Breaks. A lunch break that is not paid is time you did not work, so it does not belong in the hours total — but it does belong in the entry, marked unpaid. If the same break is sometimes paid and sometimes not, that difference alone can be a whole day of pay over a month.

Split shifts. Two blocks in one day (morning and evening, with a long gap) are still one day. Record both blocks and the gap, otherwise the total quietly becomes the length of the day instead of the hours you worked.

Overtime. Hours beyond the agreed day are not the same hours as the first ones. Keep them in a separate field or a separate line, even when the rate is identical today: when the multiplier changes later, the record still shows exactly where the threshold was crossed. The arithmetic for multipliers is in the hourly wage calculator guide.

Check the total against the pay

The point of tracking is the comparison. At the end of the month, put your total hours next to the amount that arrived. Three outcomes, three actions:

The numbers match. Keep the record anyway; it is what makes next month's comparison quick.

They differ slightly. Usually a rounding convention or an unpaid break. Find the rule, write it down once, and apply it every month — the aim is a method, not a one-off correction.

They differ by days. Now the record does its real job: send the monthly summary before the next payday, with the days listed. If wages are already late, this is the order of steps to take.

Paper, spreadsheet or app

Paper never runs out of battery and needs no explanation to anyone. Its weakness is that it stays at home while your phone is with you at work, so entries slide to the weekend.

A spreadsheet is excellent if you work at a computer all day. For hourly and shift work it is usually the wrong tool: the file lives on another machine, and the entry is made hours after the shift.

A phone app wins on timing, which is the only thing that really matters. Choose one that keeps the data on your device and lets you export it, so your record is yours and not locked behind an account.

Frequently asked

What is a work hours tracker?
Any record of the hours you actually worked, written as you work them. Paper, a spreadsheet or a phone app all count — the entries matter, not the tool.
How often should I update it?
Every day, at the end of the shift, plus a five-minute weekly check for missed days.
Should unpaid breaks be recorded?
Yes, and marked unpaid. Paid and unpaid breaks produce different totals, and that difference is a classic end-of-month dispute.
I already lost a month — can I rebuild it?
Partly. Use messages, bank transfers and shift patterns, and mark what you are unsure about instead of guessing precisely.
Does MD Work track automatically?
