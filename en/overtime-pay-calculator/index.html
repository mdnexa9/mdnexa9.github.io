Guide · Overtime

Overtime pay, worked out in numbers.

Overtime is not a feeling at the end of a long week; it is a rate multiplied by a number of hours. Once you know which rate applied and how many hours were worked, the sum takes ten seconds. The difficulty is almost always in the second part — knowing the hours.

Three numbers, in this order

Every overtime calculation uses the same three figures. Get them in the wrong order and the total drifts by exactly the amount people argue about.

1. The base hourly rate. This is your normal rate, not your overtime rate. If you are paid by the hour, you already have it. If you are paid by the day, divide the day rate by the paid hours in a normal day. If you are paid monthly, divide by the agreed monthly hours — the hourly wage calculator guide shows both the 173-hour and the 4.33-week methods.

2. The multiplier. The number your contract or the local rules apply to extra hours: 1.25, 1.5 (“time and a half”), 2 (“double time”), or 1 for a flat rate. Ask once, write the answer down, and use the same figure every month.

3. The overtime hours. Only the hours beyond the normal day or week, and only the ones you can actually point to. Breaks, travel time and waiting time each have their own rule; keep them separate instead of folding them into one number.

The formula and three worked examples

The formula is short: overtime pay = base hourly rate × multiplier × overtime hours.

Time and a half. Rate 12.00 an hour, ten extra hours in the month. 12.00 × 1.5 = 18.00; 18.00 × 10 = 180.00. If those ten hours had been paid at the normal rate they would be 120.00, so the premium is 60.00 — the part that is easy to lose.

Double time. Same rate, six hours on a Sunday at 2×. 12.00 × 2 = 24.00; 24.00 × 6 = 144.00.

A day rate with overtime. Day rate 96.00 for an 8-hour day, so the equivalent rate is 12.00. Two extra hours at 1.25 are 12.00 × 1.25 × 2 = 30.00 on top of the day.

Notice what changes between the three examples: only the multiplier and the hours. That is why a single record with a separate overtime column works for every contract — you are not rewriting the past when the rate changes, you are multiplying a stored number.

Where overtime calculations go wrong

Multiplying the wrong rate. Applying 1.5 twice — once to get the overtime rate and again when adding it to the total — inflates the sum and makes the whole record look unreliable. Multiply once, then add.

Counting a paid break as overtime. A paid break is working time; an unpaid one is not. If the break is paid, the hour is already in the normal total, so adding it again pays it twice.

Rounding before multiplying. Round the hours to the nearest quarter if that is the local convention, then multiply. Rounding the money and then multiplying loses a few cents per shift, which is a day of pay over a year.

Losing the threshold. Overtime starts somewhere — after eight hours, after forty, after six days. If the record shows only a daily total of eleven hours, nobody can tell how many of them crossed the line. Keep normal hours and extra hours apart, even when the rate is the same today.

The record that proves the hours

Overtime is the part of a payslip most often disputed, and the dispute is almost never about arithmetic — it is about whether the hours existed. Three habits settle that:

Write it on the day. Date, start, end, break, and a mark for the hours beyond the normal day. Two minutes at the end of the shift.

Keep the request. The message asking you to stay late, the shift note, the group chat: that is the proof the extra hours were asked for, not invented.

Send a monthly summary. The total of normal hours and overtime hours, before payday. A number sent in advance is hard to argue with later; a number produced after a complaint looks like a reconstruction.

Frequently asked

What is time and a half?
The normal hourly rate × 1.5. At 12.00 an hour, an overtime hour is 18.00.
I am paid by the day — how do I get an hourly rate?
Divide the day rate by the paid hours in a normal day, then apply the multiplier.
Do breaks count as overtime?
Only if you stayed available and working; an unpaid meal break does not count.
My employer pays a flat rate for extra hours — is that wrong?
It depends on the contract and local rules. What you can always do is record the hours and the rate that was actually paid.
How far back can I reconstruct?
Use messages, transport tickets and bank transfers, and mark what is uncertain. A modest accurate record is worth more than a precise guess.
