Guide · Unpaid wages

When wages go unpaid, the order matters.

Unpaid wages are rarely solved by a louder message. They are solved by a clear list of days, a calm written request, and a record that does not change its numbers. Here is the order that keeps a claim strong from the first day to the last.

Step 1 — confirm the facts before you accuse

Write down what you believe you are owed: the dates worked, the hours or days, the rate that applied, the amount you received (if any), and the date payment was promised. Do that before you send anything. Half of all “unpaid wages” cases turn out to be a misread payslip, a payment split across two transfers, or a rate that changed — and finding that out at the start saves you a conversation you cannot take back.

Then check the boring possibilities: a different bank account, a payment dated for the next cycle, a deduction you agreed to. If the gap survives all of that, you have a real question and a clean number.

Step 2 — ask once, in writing, with the numbers

A single message that states facts and asks one question works better than ten that express frustration. Keep it to five lines:

The period: “I worked from 3 to 28 November, 19 days, 152 hours.”

The agreed rate: “At 12.00 per hour, as agreed on 2 November.”

What arrived: “I received 900.00 on 5 December.”

The difference: “That leaves 924.00 outstanding.”

The ask: “Could you confirm the payment date?”

Attach your summary — one image or one PDF. Written, dated, polite and specific: this message is the document that every later step will refer to, and it is much stronger than a phone call nobody can quote.

Step 3 — build the evidence file while it is fresh

Keep one folder, digital or paper, with: your dated record of days and hours; the agreed rate (contract, offer message, or written confirmation); messages about shifts and hours; bank statements showing previous payments and amounts; payslips you received; and the names of colleagues who worked the same days.

The record of days is the piece most people are missing. Everything else usually exists somewhere — the hours exist only in memory. A work hours tracker is exactly that file, and it is why the entry made on the day is worth more than the story told a month later.

Step 4 — escalate in the right order

Your employer, in writing, once more. If the first message produced no date, send a shorter one: the same numbers, a stated deadline, and what you will do next. That is not a threat; it is a sequence.

The labour authority in your country. Most countries have an inspectorate, an ombudsman or a wage-guarantee body that handles exactly this. Bring the folder, not the story.

A union or an advice service. If you are a member, this is what membership is for. If you are not, free advice centres and migrant-worker organisations often handle wage claims.

Small claims or a labour court. Usually the last step, and usually the one most people never reach — because the file settles the question first.

Ask about deadlines early. In many places the clock starts on the date the pay was due, and waiting a year can quietly remove the option.

What weakens a claim

Numbers that change. If your total was 152 hours in the first message and 148 in the second, both become doubtful. Recalculate before you send, then keep the number.

No dates. “I worked most of November” cannot be checked by anyone, including someone who wants to pay you.

Mixing earned and received. Two separate columns, always. The gap between them is the claim.

Undocumented cash. If part of the pay was cash, write it down the same day, with the amount and who handed it over. It still counts — but only if it exists in writing.

Waiting in silence. A polite message on day three is a normal business question. The same message on day ninety sounds like a dispute.

This page is general information, not legal advice. Rules on notice, deductions and claim deadlines differ by country; a labour authority, union or adviser can tell you what applies where you work. If the disagreement is about how the hours were calculated rather than whether they were paid, the wage calculation guide shows the arithmetic, and the work time record guide shows what to keep.

Frequently asked

What do I do first?
Confirm the facts and the exact number, then send one calm written message with your summary attached and ask for a payment date.
What evidence helps?
A dated record of days and hours, the agreed rate, shift messages, bank statements, payslips, and the names of colleagues.
How long do I have?
It varies by country and claim type, and deadlines can be short. Ask a labour authority or adviser early.
Is this legal advice?
No — it is general information about records and the order of steps. For your case, consult a qualified adviser.
Should I keep working while it is unresolved?
That is your decision and depends on your contract and circumstances; an advice service can tell you what protects you locally.
