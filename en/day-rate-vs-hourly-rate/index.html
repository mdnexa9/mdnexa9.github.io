Guide · Pay rates

A day rate and an hourly rate are not the same number.

“Ninety a day” and “twelve an hour” sound comparable and are not. The only honest comparison is the effective hourly rate: the money divided by the hours the work actually takes, including the ones nobody counts.

Convert first, compare second

To turn a day rate into an hourly rate, divide it by the hours the day really takes — travel between sites included if the agreement says so, breaks excluded if they are unpaid.

Example A. 96.00 for an eight-hour day = 12.00 an hour.

Example B. The same 96.00 for a day that runs ten hours = 9.60 an hour.

Same rate on the invoice, a fifth less money per hour. This is why two people on “the same day rate” can be earning very different amounts, and why the hours have to be written down before the comparison means anything.

The break-even hours

There is a single number that tells you which arrangement is better: day rate ÷ hourly rate. It is the number of hours at which both pay the same.

Day rate 96.00, hourly offer 12.00: break-even is 8 hours. If your days usually finish in seven, the day rate pays more. If they usually run nine, the hourly rate pays more. If they vary, the answer is “whichever fits the average week”, which is why the record of a normal month is worth more than the offer letter.

The same logic works in reverse for a monthly salary: divide by the hours you are actually at work, not by the hours in the contract, and you get the figure that lets you compare a salaried job with a day-rate one.

Where the day rate quietly loses

Short days. A day rate protects you when the day is short — if it is paid in full. Many agreements cut it for a half day or a four-hour day, and then the day rate is simply an hourly rate with a rounding rule that favours the other side.

Waiting time. Materials late, a site closed, a client who does not show: on a day rate you are usually paid for the day; on an hourly rate you are paid for the hours you can prove. Both are fair, but only one of them is written down by default.

Long days. The day that starts at seven and ends at seven is the classic case. On a day rate the extra four hours are free unless overtime is agreed separately; on an hourly rate they are the best-paid part of the month.

Frequency of work. A generous day rate on two days a week can pay less than a modest hourly rate on five. Compare the month, not the day.

Where the hourly rate quietly loses

Fragmented days. Two hours here, three there: the hourly rate pays exactly for what you worked, which is correct and also means an interrupted day earns almost nothing.

Administration. Every hour has to be recorded and defended. On a day rate the argument is about whether the day existed; on an hourly rate it is about every single hour, so the record has to be better, not worse.

Availability. Being reachable and ready is not the same as working, and it is rarely paid by the hour unless the agreement says so.

The practical conclusion is unglamorous: neither rate is better in general. What decides it is the distribution of your hours — and the only way to know that distribution is to record it. One month of records settles the question

If you are choosing between two offers, ask for the hours of a normal month at the place — or use your own last month. Add them up, divide the day rate by the average day length, and compare the two totals. Ten minutes of arithmetic against a decision that lasts a year.

Frequently asked

How do I compare a day rate with an hourly rate?
Divide the day rate by the hours the day actually takes. 96.00 over 10 hours is 9.60 an hour.
What is the break-even point?
Day rate ÷ hourly rate. 96.00 ÷ 12.00 = 8 hours: shorter days favour the day rate, longer days the hourly rate.
Is a day rate better for short days?
Only when a short day is paid in full. Check how half days are paid before assuming it.
How is waiting time paid?
It depends on whether you had to stay available. Record it separately, with start and end times.
What if the rate changes during the month?
Record the rate that applied to each day, then total. A single monthly rate would hide the change.
