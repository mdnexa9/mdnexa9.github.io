Guide · Hourly wage calculator

An hourly wage calculator you can do in your head.

Every wage calculation is the same four steps: count the hours, subtract the hours you did not work, multiply by the rate, then handle overtime separately. Once you know the order, you can check any payslip in two minutes — and spot the month where the hours were counted wrong.

The four formulas

Pay = hours worked × hourly rate. Everything else is a correction to this line.

Hours worked = time at work − unpaid breaks. A nine-hour shift with a one-hour unpaid lunch is eight paid hours. Multiply the shift length instead and every day of the month is overstated by an hour.

Overtime pay = overtime hours × rate × multiplier. The multiplier applies to the hours that qualify, never to the whole total.

Monthly estimate = weekly pay × 4.33. A month averages 4.33 weeks, not four. Four weeks is the most common reason a careful calculation still does not match the payslip at the end of the month.

Worked example: a normal week

Rate: 12.00 per hour. The week has five shifts of eight paid hours.

40 hours × 12.00 = 480.00 for the week.

Now the same week with reality in it: one shift ran two hours longer, and two days had a half-hour unpaid break counted twice by mistake.

Base hours: 40 × 12.00 = 480.00.

Overtime: 2 hours at time and a half → 2 × 12.00 × 1.5 = 36.00.

Total gross for the week: 516.00. The monthly estimate at 4.33 weeks is 516.00 × 4.33 = 2,234.28, which is a planning figure, not a promise — real months contain five-shift and four-shift weeks.

Half days and daily rates

If you are paid by the day, the hourly equivalent is your day rate divided by the paid hours in a normal day: 96.00 for an eight-hour day is 12.00 per hour. A half day is half the day rate — 48.00 — and part of a day is proportional to the hours actually worked.

Record which unit you are paid in before you start multiplying. Mixing a day rate with an hourly calculation is how a full day quietly becomes eight hours at the wrong rate. The daily pay guide covers the same arithmetic from the day-rate side, and the tracker guide shows which fields to keep so the numbers exist at all.

Rounding, breaks and other quiet differences

Rounding. Some employers round each shift to the nearest quarter hour, others round the week. Either rule is workable; what is not workable is applying it silently in one direction only. Write the rule down once, and calculate your own figure the same way.

Breaks. Ask one question: is the break paid? If it is unpaid, subtract it from the hours. If nobody can answer, keep the break in your record as a separate line so both versions of the total are visible.

Travel and waiting time. Time spent waiting to start, or travelling between sites during the day, is often paid — but the rules differ by contract and country. Record it separately and let the contract decide, instead of merging it into the total and losing the distinction.

Gross versus net. The multiplication gives gross pay. Net pay depends on tax, contributions and deductions, so the number that arrives in the bank is always smaller. Compare your calculation with the gross line on the payslip, then check the net separately.

Three errors that repeat every month

Multiplying shift length instead of paid hours; applying the overtime multiplier to the entire week instead of the extra hours; and using four weeks for a month. Each one moves the total by more than most people expect — the first by roughly 12% on a nine-hour shift with a lunch break, the last by about 8%.

The fix is not a better calculator. It is a record of the days, so the calculation has real hours to work with. A work time record takes a minute a day and makes all four formulas checkable.

Frequently asked

Pay from an hourly wage?
Hours actually worked × rate, with overtime hours on their own multiplier. Subtract unpaid breaks from the hours first.
Hourly wage to monthly?
Weekly pay × 4.33. It is an estimate: months are not four weeks long and paid leave changes the total.
What is time and a half?
A 1.5 multiplier on the normal rate, applied only to overtime hours. 12.00 becomes 18.00 per extra hour.
Net or gross?
The multiplication gives gross. Net depends on tax and contributions, so always compare against the gross line on the payslip.
What if my payslip does not match?
Compare day by day, not totals. One wrong classification — a half day logged as a full day, or a break counted twice — explains most differences.
